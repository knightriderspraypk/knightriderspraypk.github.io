Where Do Fake Delay Sprays Come From?

Understanding the supply chain helps you avoid the traps. Most fakes in Pakistan come from three sources. First, refilled bottles: empty original bottles collected from trash or salons, refilled with cheap desensitizing liquid, resealed, and sold as new. These are the scariest because the packaging looks perfect. Second, lookalike brands: bottles with near-identical names and label designs — "Vega" instead of "Viga", slightly-off logos — made to fool a quick glance. Third, grey-market dilutions: genuine concentrate cut with filler to stretch one bottle into three. All three are sold through the same channels: anonymous WhatsApp sellers, roadside "medical stores" with no receipts, and marketplace listings with stolen product photos.

Red Flag #1: The Price Makes No Sense

This is the fastest filter. A genuine Viga 1 Million spray sells for around ₨2,499; Knight Rider spray goes for about ₨1,900. If someone offers you either for ₨800 "wholesale", it's fake — full stop. Real sellers have real costs: import, packaging, COD logistics. Nobody sells the genuine article at a 60% discount out of generosity. Discounts of 10–20% during sales are normal; anything beyond that is a warning siren. The same logic applies to suspiciously cheap Big Bull spray or Vimax timing spray listings.

Red Flag #2: Sloppy Packaging Details

Hold the box and look closely. Genuine delay sprays have sharp, high-resolution printing; fakes often show blurry text, off-center logos, or colors that are slightly wrong — a red that's too orange, a silver that looks grey. Check spelling with a magnifying eye: counterfeiters routinely slip up on "delay", "Germany", or the brand name itself. Compare against the product photos on the official store listing — the Viga 8 Million and Procomil pages show exactly what the real box looks like. Also feel the materials: real boxes use sturdy card and the bottle feels solid; fakes often use flimsy cardboard and a pump that sticks or leaks.

Red Flag #3: No Batch Code or Expiry Date

Every legitimate personal-care spray carries a batch/lot number and an expiry date, usually printed or embossed on the bottle or box — not on a sticker slapped over something else. If the bottle has neither, walk away. If the expiry date looks altered, smudged, or printed in a different font from the rest of the label, that's a classic refill tell: someone wiped the old date and printed a new one. Genuine stock from a proper store will have clear, consistent markings. When your parcel arrives, this is the first thing to check before you pay the COD rider.

Red Flag #4: The Seller Acts Shady

Legitimate sellers behave like businesses: they have a real website with a real address, they show actual photos of their own stock, they offer Cash on Delivery, and they answer questions about batch and expiry. Fake sellers behave like ghosts: WhatsApp-only contact, stock photos stolen from Google, no website, advance payment demanded via EasyPaisa with no receipt, and they vanish the moment you ask for the batch code. Another tell — they pressure you: "last piece", "offer ends tonight", "don't tell anyone this price." A real store selling Eros delay spray or Maxman spray doesn't need theatrics.

Red Flag #5: The Liquid Itself Is Wrong

If you've used a genuine spray before, you'll notice. Fakes often smell strongly of alcohol or perfume (real delay sprays are usually near-odorless or mildly scented), feel watery instead of slightly viscous, or the pump delivers an uneven, sputtering mist. The effect is wrong too — either nothing happens at all, or there's an intense burning/tingling that the original never caused. If any of that happens, stop using it immediately and don't try to "get your money's worth" out of a suspicious bottle. Your skin will thank you.

The Buy-Original Checklist (Run This Every Time)

  • Price check: compare against the official store — within ~20% is normal, 50% off is fake.
  • Seller check: real website, real contact details, COD offered, discreet packaging promised.
  • Photo check: product photos look like actual stock photos of the item, not Google images.
  • On arrival: inspect the seal, batch code, and expiry date before paying the rider.
  • Packaging check: sharp printing, correct spelling, sturdy box, working pump.
  • Slug check: buy the current listing — e.g. Knight Rider's live page is knight-rider-delay-spray-2; the old slug without "-2" is dead, and sellers still advertising it are selling old or fake stock.

What To Do If You Already Bought a Fake

First, stop using it — don't try to finish the bottle. Second, document everything: photos of the packaging, the seller's number, payment receipts. Third, if you paid a business (not an individual), ask for a refund with the photos; reputable stores will make it right. Fourth, report the seller — on marketplaces, leave an honest review with photos so the next buyer is warned. And finally, replace it with a verified original from a store that shows batch and expiry info upfront. The ₨1,650–₨2,499 you spend on a genuine Big Bull or Viga 1 Million is cheaper than the regret of a mystery bottle.
